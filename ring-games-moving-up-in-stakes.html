Moving Up in Stakes

How to know when you're ready, and what changes when you get there.

Every ring game player eventually asks the same question. The table you started at feels comfortable. You know what you're doing. So when do you move up?

There's no rule about this, but there are better and worse answers.

The wrong reasons to move up

You're bored. Boredom is a real signal, but it usually means you want more action, not tougher opposition. Moving up gets you both, and only one of those is what you were after. Try a different game type or a shorter-handed table first.

You just won a big pot. One session doesn't tell you much. A single good run can happen to anyone at any level.

You want to win faster. Bigger stakes mean bigger swings in both directions. Moving up to speed things up usually just makes the downswings arrive sooner.

The right reasons

The decisions have stopped being hard. This is the real one. When you can predict what your opponents will do before they do it, and you're rarely unsure what to do yourself, the table has stopped teaching you anything.

You've played enough hands to know it isn't luck. A few good sessions is noise. A consistent pattern over a lot of hands is information.

You have the chips to sit properly. Covered below, and it matters more than people expect.

Sizing your stack for the next level

The point from Lesson 2 applies here directly: what matters is your stack in big blinds, not the raw chip count.

To sit with a standard 100 big blinds, you need 100 times the big blind at that level. Work it out before you sit:

  • 10/20 table — 2,000 chips for a full buy-in
  • 25/50 table — 5,000 chips
  • 1000/2000 table — 200,000 chips
  • 200000/400000 table — 40,000,000 chips

Notice how fast that scales. Each step up isn't a small increase, it's a different order of magnitude.

Bring more than one buy-in. Sitting with your last chips means every decision carries weight it shouldn't. If losing a stack means you're done for the session, you'll play scared, and playing scared costs more than the chips do. A comfortable rule is enough for several full buy-ins at your level before you sit.

What actually changes at higher stakes

Fewer players are giving chips away. At low stakes, a chunk of your profit comes from opponents making obvious mistakes. That shrinks as you move up. The same play that crushed a small game may only break even in a bigger one.

Bluffs work more often, and get caught more often. Better opponents fold more when they're beaten, which makes bluffing more effective. They also call more when they think you're weak. Both directions get sharper.

Position matters even more. Skilled opponents punish you harder when you're out of position, and give up less when you're in it. Everything in Lesson 3 gets amplified.

The swings feel bigger. Same variance, larger numbers. If a bad session at the new level would genuinely bother you, that's a sign to wait.

A sensible way to do it

Move up one level at a time. Skipping levels means facing better opposition and unfamiliar stack depths at once, and it's hard to tell which one is causing your problems.

Try a short session at the new level, then go back down and compare. If the bigger game felt manageable, stay. If you spent the whole session unsure what to do, you've learned something useful and you can go back and keep working.

There's no penalty for moving back down. Plenty of players sit at several levels depending on the day, the table, and how they're feeling. Playing well at a smaller stake beats playing badly at a bigger one, every time.
