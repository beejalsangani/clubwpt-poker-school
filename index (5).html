When to Leave

The one skill that only exists in ring games.

A tournament ends when it ends. You bust, or you win, and either way the decision is made for you.

A ring game never ends. It runs until you decide to stand up. That decision is a real skill, and almost nobody works on it.

The bad reasons players stay

"I'm stuck and I need to get it back." This is the most expensive thought in poker. The table doesn't know you're down. Your chips have no memory. The next hand pays the same whether you've been winning or losing all night, and playing longer because you're behind is how a bad session becomes a much worse one.

"I'm running good, I should keep going." Better instinct, still not a reason on its own. Being up doesn't make you sharper. If anything, a big win makes players loose.

"I just sat down." Not a reason to stay if the table is wrong for you.

The real reasons to leave

Your decisions are getting slower or sloppier. This is the honest one. When you notice you're calling without really thinking, or you can't remember what happened two hands ago, you're done. Fatigue costs more than any single bad beat.

You're frustrated. Tilt isn't always dramatic. It's usually just a small, quiet drop in patience. If you find yourself playing hands you'd normally fold because you're bored or annoyed, stand up.

The table changed. The loose player left. Two strong regulars sat down. The game you chose isn't the game you're in anymore. Leave, or move.

You hit your time. Deciding in advance how long you'll play removes the decision from the moment when you're least equipped to make it.

Set the rules before you sit

The reason this is hard is that you make the decision while inside the session, when you're tired, frustrated, or riding a run. Nobody thinks clearly there.

So decide first:

  • How long am I playing?
  • What would tell me the table has gone bad?
  • What does "I'm not playing well anymore" look like for me?

Write it down if you have to. The point is that the decision gets made by the version of you that isn't tilted.

The upside of a format with no ending

The flip side of "it never ends" is that you never have to force anything. There's no bubble, no pay jump, no blind level pushing you into a hand you don't want. Every hand you play is one you chose to play.

That's the whole appeal of ring games. Use it.
